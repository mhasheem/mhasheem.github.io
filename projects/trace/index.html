When AI Work Becomes Official

Locating Authority in AI-Assisted Coursework

Abstract

Human oversight is often proposed as a safeguard in AI-supported decisions, but having a person involved does not always clarify what they are responsible for. In higher education, generative AI can draft course materials, suggest grades and provide feedback, raising questions about when these outputs should become part of a course or affect students.

We report a formative mixed-methods study combining surveys of 67 instructors and 94 students with interviews with six instructors and seven students. Willingness to delegate work to AI did not mean willingness to give up control over academic decisions. Among instructors, 43 of 67 wanted the ability to review and edit AI outputs, and 41 wanted final academic decisions to remain with instructors. Students rated instructor-reviewed grading more favorably than AI-only grading, yet 41 of the 64 who considered reviewed grading fair or partly fair still wanted additional safeguards. Participants' concerns went beyond correctness to whether the evidence was appropriate, who was responsible, and whether a decision could be reconsidered.

We synthesize these findings into the Coursework Authorization Model, which distinguishes what information AI may use as evidence, which AI-produced outputs become part of a course, and when those outputs may affect students, and illustrate it through TRACE, a course-grounded prototype that makes authorization decisions explicit and inspectable.

The Coursework Authorization Model

Three decisions, not one "human in the loop"

01 · Source

What counts as evidence

Which available course materials the AI may rely on for a given task.

02 · Artifact

What becomes official

Which AI-produced version is adopted as part of the course.

03 · Consequence

When it affects students

When adopted work may be released or applied in a way that affects a student.

Meaningful oversight depends not simply on reviewing AI outputs, but on clarifying what is being authorized, who remains responsible, and how consequential decisions can be reconsidered.

The prototype

TRACE makes every authorization explicit

TRACE is a course-grounded assistant in which each step from course material to student consequence is a separate, recorded decision. It is now publicly deployed as Fazah.
